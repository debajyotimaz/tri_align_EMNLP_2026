In brief

Multilingual encoders such as mBERT and XLM-R are widely used for code-mixed text, but it is unclear how their representations of code-mixed sentences relate to the two languages being mixed. We build a parallel corpus of 21,139 English, Hindi (Devanagari) and Romanized Hinglish sentence triples and probe six encoders with retrieval, CKA, token-level saliency and entropy-based uncertainty.

  1. Code-mixed text sits on the periphery

    Off-the-shelf encoders align English and Hindi best. Code-mixed sentences are weakly anchored to either language: EN↔HI > EN↔CM > HI↔CM.

  2. Adaptation trades one gap for another

    Continued pretraining on Hinglish pulls code-mixed text toward English, but English–Hindi alignment drops. Models read code-mixed text through an English-dominant subspace, while native-script Hindi reduces uncertainty.

  3. Trilingual alignment restores balance

    A post-training loss that aligns all three pairs at once lifts CLAS for every model and improves cross-lingual consistency on sentiment and hate speech detection.

Trilingual post-training alignment

We keep each encoder's original objective (MLM + NSP for mBERT, MLM for XLM-R) and add a cosine alignment term over the normalized [CLS] embeddings of each parallel triple (e, h, c):

L = Lbase + λ · Lalign,   λ = 0.05
Lalign = ⅓ Σ(u,v) ∈ {(e,h), (e,c), (h,c)}  1/B Σi (1 − ui⊤vi)

To score how evenly a model aligns the three pairs, we introduce the Cross-Lingual Alignment Score: CLAS = MeanAcc − DirBias − SetupStd. It rewards high retrieval accuracy and penalizes direction asymmetry and imbalance across EN↔HI, EN↔CM and HI↔CM.
